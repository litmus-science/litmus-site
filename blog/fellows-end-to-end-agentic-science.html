Meet the Fellows Running End-to-End Agentic Science

Joy-Yan Lam, Alexander Reading, and Daniel Pascal are the pilot cohort of the Hypothesis-to-Evidence Grant. Each will take a computational result from K-Dense Web to a real wet-lab measurement run through Litmus Science.

By Litmus Science & K-Dense

Models can now propose a mechanism, rank candidate compounds, or predict a binding site in an afternoon. What they can’t do is tell you whether the prediction holds up at the bench. The Hypothesis-to-Evidence Grant, which we run with K-Dense, exists to fund that last step.

Our pilot cohort is two projects: one from Joy-Yan Lam, PhD, and one from the team of Alexander Reading and Daniel Pascal. Both follow the same path. The hypothesis and protocol are worked out in K-Dense Web, K-Dense’s AI co-scientist. Once the protocol is approved, it moves over the native MCP connection into Litmus, where we gather lab quotes and run the experiment. Nobody has to copy a protocol into an email or chase vendors by hand.

What the grant covers
  • Up to $5,000 of wet-lab work per project, sourced through Litmus Science
  • Fellows keep ownership of their data and intellectual property
  • Each project is published as a public case study once results are in, with the fellows credited by name

For background on how the two platforms fit together, see K-Dense Web and Litmus Science: Closing the Loop from Hypothesis to Experiment.

Joy-Yan Lam, PhD — University of Hong Kong

Sponsored project

Finding the disease-modifying compounds in a Chinese medicine decoction for Parkinson’s disease

Lam is a Research Assistant Professor at the Centre for Virology, Vaccinology and Therapeutics at the University of Hong Kong, where his main work is a protein-based nasal vaccine for respiratory viruses. His grant project is a separate question with a personal origin. A traditional Chinese medicine formula used for Parkinson’s has a documented history of reversing symptoms, including in a close relative of his. He wants to know which molecules in it do the work.

The need is large. Over 10 million people have Parkinson’s today, a number projected to pass 25 million by 2050, and no approved therapy slows the disease.

Lam’s hypothesis is that specific molecules in the formula reverse Parkinson’s motor symptoms. In K-Dense Web, he will turn mass spectrometry data on the decoction into a map of compounds and their protein targets, build a protein–protein interaction network, and pick candidates from its hub genes. The lab work is an in vitro screen of that shortlist, read out on neuronal survival and inflammatory gene expression.

A null result is still informative here. If nothing in the extract is active, the likely explanations are that the active compound isn’t in the water-soluble fraction, or that it breaks down during processing.

Alexander Reading & Daniel Pascal — MIT

Grant project

A first test of targeted protein degradation against fungi

Reading and Pascal are Biological Engineering PhD students at MIT working as a team. Pascal’s thesis covers engineered therapeutic probiotics, including a strain that hunts down and kills microbial pathogens. Reading’s thesis is on genetically engineering yeast, with a focus on how proteins are translated.

Their target is a serious gap in medicine. Invasive fungal infections are estimated to kill about 2.5 million people every year, clinicians have just four classes of antifungal drugs, and resistance is appearing against every one of them.

Targeted protein degradation began in cancer research. A degrader molecule tags a chosen protein and hands it to the cell’s own disposal machinery, which destroys it. The approach has since spread to viral and bacterial targets, but nobody has applied it to fungi.

The first requirement is binding. Structural analysis in K-Dense Web showed that the pocket where a known molecule binds a human protein is largely conserved in the fungal version, even though the rest of the protein has drifted over evolutionary time. Through Litmus, they will now measure how strongly that molecule actually binds the fungal protein. If it binds, and binds tightly, that opens the door to designing a new class of antifungals.

What happens next

The fellows are finalizing the evidence base and protocols in K-Dense Web. Litmus will then return lab quotes and run the approved experiments. We’ll publish each project as a case study once the data are in.
